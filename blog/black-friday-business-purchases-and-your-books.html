Black Friday for Business Owners: A Real Deal, Without the Messy Books

Y
Yurii TomashevskyiNovember 25, 2026 · 7 min read

Black Friday lands on November 27 this year, with Cyber Monday following on November 30. For many business owners it is a genuinely good time to buy: a new laptop, software you have been watching, supplies for the year ahead. It is also the week when more money leaves the account in a few days than in a typical month, often through quick purchases made on a phone.

None of this is a problem. A little planning just keeps a good deal a good deal, and keeps your books clear when you sit down in December. This guide covers how to decide what is worth buying, how to tell whether a discount is real, and how to record it all so it does not turn into homework in March. It is written for self-employed people and small business owners across Canada.

Why this week matters for your books

Sale weekends combine several things that make bookkeeping harder: many small purchases, a mix of personal and business items, receipts that arrive only by email, orders from foreign websites, and payments split across cards or instalment plans. None of these is difficult on its own. Together, they are the reason a quiet December reconciliation sometimes turns into a long evening of “what was this charge?”

A few habits, set before the sales begin, solve most of it.

Step 1: Decide before the sale starts

The easiest way to avoid unnecessary buying is to settle what you need while you are calm.

  • Write a short list of things the business genuinely needs in the next six to twelve months, with the price you expect to pay at normal times. If it is not on the list, it needs a good reason.
  • Set a ceiling for the whole weekend. Choose an amount your cash flow handles comfortably, not an amount that merely fits on the card.
  • Use a 24-hour pause for anything not on the list. Put it in the cart, close the tab and look again tomorrow. Many deals look less necessary after a night, and the truly good ones are often still there.

Step 2: Is it really a discount?

A big red “50% off” tells you the seller wants your attention. It does not tell you the price is good. In Canada, advertising a “regular price” that was never real is not allowed under the Competition Act, and the Competition Bureau has warned businesses about it around Black Friday. A seller generally has to show that the higher price is genuine: that more than half of the sales were made at that price or higher, or that the product was offered in good faith at that price for a substantial period.

You do not need to audit anyone, but a few quick checks help:

  • Look at the price history. Price-tracking tools and browser extensions show what an item cost over the previous months. If it was the same price last month, the “sale” is simply the price.
  • Compare two or three sellers. The same product is often cheaper elsewhere on a normal Tuesday.
  • Compare the full cost. Include shipping, taxes, and currency conversion if you are paying in US dollars. A cheaper listing price is not always a cheaper total.
  • Be wary of urgency. A countdown timer or “only two left” message is a sales technique, not information about value.
  • Check what you are agreeing to. A “discounted first year” on software can renew at the full price, and a free trial may start billing in the new year.

If you do suspect a fake discount, the Competition Bureau accepts reports. And if a deal still looks good after these checks, enjoy it.

Step 3: Keep business and personal separate

This is the most useful habit of the week. When a sale is on, it is easy to add a personal item to a business order, or to buy a gift on the business card because it is the one in your hand.

  • Pay for business purchases from the business account or card, and personal ones from your personal accounts.
  • If a personal item ends up on the business card, tell your bookkeeper or mark it in your records right away. It is not a business expense. For a sole proprietor it is recorded as an owner draw, and in a corporation it is usually treated as a shareholder loan or a personal amount owed back to the company.
  • Mixed-use items, such as a phone or a computer used for both work and home, can be partly business. Decide on a reasonable business-use percentage and write down how you arrived at it.

Fixing this in December costs a few minutes. Fixing it after a year of mixed charges costs a lot more.

Step 4: Save the right receipt

For online orders, the order confirmation email is usually your receipt. It is worth saving it as a PDF into your business folder the same day, because links expire and emails get buried.

If you are registered for GST/HST, claiming input tax credits needs proper documentation. The CRA sets different levels depending on the amount of the purchase:

  • Under $30: the seller's name, the date and the total amount, in a form that lets you work out the tax.
  • $30 to under $150: the above, plus the seller's GST/HST registration number and the tax amount, or a statement that tax is included.
  • $150 or more: all of the above, plus your business name, the payment terms and a description of what was bought.

Not every seller shows these details on a confirmation email, and sellers outside Canada often do not charge Canadian GST/HST at all, which means there is no credit to claim. The expense itself can still be recorded; it just comes without a tax credit. If you are unsure whether a receipt is enough, ask before you rely on it.

Step 5: Big purchases and subscriptions deserve a quick think

Equipment. A computer, a camera or a piece of machinery is generally treated as an asset and deducted gradually over time through capital cost allowance, rather than all at once in the year of purchase. There are special first-year rules that can help, and they depend on the type of item and your situation. For anything sizeable, a short question to your accountant before buying is worthwhile.

Annual software plans. A plan paid in November often covers most of next year. That is perfectly reasonable, and it is good to let your bookkeeper know so the expense is placed in the right period.

Stock for resale. If your business sells products, a bulk purchase at a discount can be smart. Remember that the cash is spent now while the sales come later, and that unsold stock needs to be counted at year-end.

Step 6: Think about how you pay

A discount only helps if it does not strain your cash. A few things to keep in mind:

  • Credit cards: if you will not pay the balance in full by the due date, the interest can erase the saving. Choose a card and a plan on purpose.
  • Buy now, pay later: instalment plans are a real obligation. Make sure each plan is recorded as an amount you owe, and that the payments are in your cash flow plan.
  • Currency: purchases in US dollars are recorded in Canadian dollars at the exchange rate applicable on the day. Your card statement shows the converted amount, which is usually what you want in the books.

After the sales: a ten-minute tidy-up

In the first week of December, set aside a short session:

  • Match every Black Friday and Cyber Monday charge to a receipt.
  • Label each one as business or personal.
  • Record returns and refunds when the money comes back, not when you ship the item.
  • Put a reminder in your calendar for any free trial or promotional subscription, a few days before it renews.
  • Cancel anything you signed up for and no longer need.

If you do this once while the details are fresh, year-end becomes a much smaller task.
